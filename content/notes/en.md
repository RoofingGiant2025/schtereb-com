# Notes on the poems — English
### One short introduction per poem. Headers are `## <n>. <Title>` with the poem's number in originals.md.

## 1. White Piece
The collection opens with a prologue of nine lines and a single figure: the wanderer. The Russian plays on one root three ways — *strannik* (wanderer), *strany* (countries), *stranen* (strange) — so the wandering is not across a map but inside “Soul, Mind and Reason.” The lines are stepped across the page like footprints, and the title’s “white” is the blank page itself: a poet declaring, before the first love poem, that he is a stranger even to himself.

## 2. A Real Dream
The first love poem is a night vision seen through glass. The moon rises, a glance stops on a girl at the window, and her eyes become cornflowers in grey mist, her lips pearls, her hair moonlit and falling to the ground. The wish to see her “without the glass” breaks the spell: the window opens, she is gone, only wind remains. Desire, illusion and the pane between them — the pattern of the whole first section is set here in six rhymed quatrains.

## 3. The Meeting
A first encounter told in one unbroken breath of twenty lines. The eyes are “harmless” yet they pierce; the girl returns in dreams and promises to love always; the speaker wakes “out of his mind” — and then, in a startlingly self-aware turn, admits it is “only a game, which, like a stanza, must find its end.” The poem knows it is a poem. Infatuation and the craft of writing about it arrive at the same moment.

## 4. * * *
Twelve stepped lines, and two reflections of the sun — one in a glass of wine, one in a soul. From the eyes the speaker wants to step into “that space” of sweetness and passion, an “accidental happiness” that he names, in the last line, hell. The paradise-hell reversal is the poem’s whole argument: love as a place one begs to enter knowing it will burn. The staggered layout makes the eye fall, line by line, into it.

## 5. I Saw You…
The beloved is addressed with the formal, capitalised *Vas* — the courtly “You” — and every stanza narrows, step by step, to that one word. Disbelief, slow surrender, prayers to the gods for “not a diamond, but You,” dread of never seeing her again: an old-fashioned suit told in a very young voice. The refrain “I cannot live like this any more” is repeated until it becomes both complaint and vow.

## 6. The Soul's Desire
Since their glances met “on a single *way*” — the English word, footnoted by the author himself — the soul has been “overgrown with love.” The poem’s charm lies in its asides: nobody “wanted to go back to Kansas,” a wink at *The Wizard of Oz*, and a rush of exclamation marks in the sixth stanza where everything the soul wanted turns out to be one person. Seven rhymed quatrains, and a footnote kept in every language.

## 7. * * *
“Probably this is called LOVE” — the word in capitals, as if tried on for the first time. What follows is an inventory of wanting: to see, to touch, to hold hands, to catch her glances, to stop time when she is near, to be unable to think when she is not. The last two stanzas turn: facing loss, the speaker can only whisper “forgive me — for everything bad” and wait, not knowing what would make him lovable. Naming the feeling turns out to be easier than keeping it.

## 8. My Dream
Moonlit nights and a girl the speaker does not know how to approach — and then, mid-poem, does not know at all. Is she blonde, “curly-white,” or “black as night,” her eyes “ash from a fire”? The dream keeps changing its details because it is a dream. Afraid that her glance will pierce him “with love’s arrow,” he decides to keep his eyes shut and not find out. The author’s odd commas-before-question-marks are kept: they are the sound of a mind hesitating.

## 9. Dream of Mine
The mirror-twin of the previous poem, its title reversed. Here the girl is fixed — golden hair, tender eyes — and far away, and the road to her “is easy.” The wish that “all people on Earth vanish for a short time,” leaving only “I and You — my Goddess,” is adolescent hyperbole delivered with complete sincerity; the flowers, the moon and the capitalised *You* belong to the same register. A dream of possession that ends, honestly, as a dream.

## 10. Romantic ballad
The English title sits over a Russian poem that tells, in quatrains, a first night of love on a riverbank: counting stars, undressing, “Eve and Adam,” the river that “washed away new sins,” a morning of happiness. What makes it a ballad rather than a boast is the tone — candid, tender, faintly astonished — and the parenthesis at the end, where the speaker looks back and allows that his first night “was even very good.” The longest and most innocent of the infatuation poems.

## 11. There Was a Time
A memory of not wanting to live, told with a twist. The speaker would have “killed himself, slowly or quickly,” had he not loved and felt “a woman’s warmth” — then, bored even with that, wanted to see blood, climbed to a roof, leapt, and did not fall. He wakes: it was a dream. The poem ends with an embrace and a vow spoken to a sleeping girl: never, “even at ninety.” The stray exclamation marks in mid-line are the author’s; they mark the places where the dream lurches.

## 12. Joyful Sorrow
The oxymoron of the title governs a simple story: a cold evening, a long walk alone “where no one knew,” thoughts only of her — and then the radiance of her figure in the distance, the embrace, a whispered “I have fallen into the lake of your love,” a kiss held long enough to count as an answer. The weather follows the heart: the clouds leave and the moon appears. A first kiss written as a rebirth, in eight plain quatrains.

## 13. Memory
The longest of the early poems moves from a grey, frozen day — everyone’s eyes “frozen without warmth,” the clouds “holding together in a solid clump” — into a private night of snow and moonlight with “my tender talisman.” Walking, star-gazing, thinking “of everything and at once of nothing,” meeting the sunrise and parting with a kiss until evening: the poem is almost a film, and its structure — public cold, then shared warmth — is its meaning.

## 14. The Soul's Longing
Twelve lines of ellipses, exclamation marks and stepped indents — the typography of a held breath. The beloved is rarely seen; “I wait? I love..! I beg..!” Love burns the soul, and the soul burns, but the speaker does not shout: “there are no words.” The one certainty is stated with a capital letter and a final exclamation: the Soul is alive, though “not free.” A small poem that reads like a heartbeat.

## 15. Innocent Angel
Three times “Say it!” — an invitation to fly away into dreams, “from beauty where even the devil goes mute.” If the angel refuses, she may stay and become like everything around her: “dirty!” The speaker will go alone, rest from “the reality of being,” and not grieve that she is not there. The poem is a choice offered and withdrawn in the same breath: imagination as refuge, and the pride of preferring it to a world that will not follow.

## 16. Congratulations
A Russian title on a Ukrainian gift-poem. White spring flowers are offered with wishes — that she never weep, that she grow “like that blossom” — and with a folk-song scene: when he rides off at dawn “on a white or a black horse,” she is not to stop him, only wave. The flowers are to be pressed in “a huge book” and, years later, found again: thyme, remembered “with no unkind word.” Love that already imagines its own memory.

## 17. I Love
“Girl, I love you” — to be said over the telephone at night, then under the moon: she the lonely star, he the crescent beside her. Halfway through, the poem stops declaring and starts thinking: nothing in the world “does not pass with time,” not sunshine, not enmity, not “the time for love.” The consolation is intensity — a sun that shines brightly enough will shine long and “drive the evil clouds from the sky.” A love poem that argues with its own transience.

## 18. I Loved
The same girl, the same telephone, one tense later. He called, met her, brought birthday flowers, never kissed her — “and then came love’s finale.” Roses wilt, thunder rolls, “you did not understand me,” the heart ached and grew quiet. Read beside *I Love*, this is the second panel of a diptych: present and past of one small story, told without ornament, ending in the flat honesty of “I no longer want to love you.”

## 19. * * *
“If you do not love me, then tell me about love.” Three quatrains of conditionals: is love the colour of cold skies or “red and hot as blood”? Is it where the sun and the fine views are, or “where you are lonely”? The speaker asks only to be shown; if love proves gentle and sincere, he will smile back at it. The question — *show me where love is* — is answered, years later in the book, by *Is It Too Late?*, where she asks it of him.

## 20. Little Delirium
The title is a coinage — a diminutive of “delirium” — and the poem is a student’s grumble: summer was good, girls and freedom and “male entertainments,” and now term begins and “none of us feels love for science.” Better not to know oneself, “but to be naked in the soul” — and then the speaker catches himself: “Oh, what am I saying — no! All this is delirium. And you’re a little delirium, and you… Ah, enough of making poems.” Loose lines, a wandering voice, and a poem that cancels itself.

## 21. The Hike
A mock-epic of a hiking trip in the Carpathians, with the leader and the cook named in full and nothing spared: heavy rucksacks and “empty heads,” sleeping mats, food that may be thick soup or thin potatoes, vodka at night, a morning throat on fire — and one stanza where the eyes open on “the enchanting beauty of the Carpathians” and the joking stops. Then the train home, “a sea of tears,” and the promise of the next hike: “don’t forget cigarettes and lunch.” The book’s fullest comic portrait of a company of friends.

## 22. Drunken Saturday
Three friends, a third-floor room, bottles from a bag. The comedy is in the detail: Andriy brings the cups and sends Valera to wash them; the first shot is chased with water; a cigarette is forbidden, then bought with another pour; “we very drunk junkies” tidily collect the butts and wash the cups before heading out. Beer by brand name, the disco, chewing gum against the vodka breath, a Sunday throat — and the assurance that next Saturday will bring “our own kind of work.” A chronicle in rhymed quatrains, sung rather than confessed.

## 23. Nothing
The strangest of the early poems. A girl lies on a bed under a moon while “we boys” watch “like the frozen shadows of the dead” — and then every fact is withdrawn: maybe it was not us, maybe not her; she resembled a boa, no, a roundworm; and now, an hour later, she has become “a princess — Princess Lewis.” Where did the name come from? “Maybe it’s not you? And I’m not me? Then where are we, if this isn’t Earth?” A vision in which identity, insult and adoration dissolve together. The title is the only stable thing in it.

## 24. Junkie's Delirium
A warm evening in a city at peace — “no one was quarrelling with his wife any more” — and two bodies under the moon, until a fly stings her ear. She goes wild “like a cat fallen from a high window,” claws out, and runs home; “little sister anguish” comes to sit with the speaker and shakes him awake; not liking to be alone, he runs after her. The title frames the anecdote as an intoxicated ramble; the poem itself is quick, comic and oddly tender.

## 25. Bitch
Summer breathes autumn, and a story ends before it is told. The girl was “a rose of the wastelands,” she stank, and when asked why she was drunk she answered in English — “If you want to be okey / Drink a vodka every day” — the couplet preserved in every version. The trochaic sing-song of a folk ballad carries brutal material: pity, disgust, a lie told out of kindness, and a farewell — “fly off, stork.” The title’s spelling, a Russianism in Ukrainian, is the author’s own.

## 26. Singing at a Funeral
Fog on the river, a little dope smoked, and a mock burial: a flower laid on her chest, a laugh, and “my dream” pushed gently into the water with a sentence left unfinished by the author’s own row of dots. Then the speaker sits down and sings — the song is printed inside the poem, in quotation marks — a hymn to hemp, which “makes dreams pleasant” and “is our strength.” What is being buried is never named. A very dark comedy, and one of the collection’s few poems with a song inside it.

## 27. Blessed Moment
A mask, and the book’s darkest one. The speaker drinks blood from a cup “like an enraged snake,” laughs “Ha-Ha” at his own relief, looks Jehovah in the eye and stays as he was — “stern in the dark, with eyes full of malice.” Not for ever, he says: first let “Great Perun,” the Slavic thunder god, send down a lightning arrow and a low bow to “his majestic Padre.” Gothic theatre played absolutely straight; the strangeness is deliberate, and the pagan and biblical names are the author’s.

## 28. Don't Sniff Lacquer!
Something has changed: the speaker no longer wants to sniff lacquer, and instead tastes spring. The world grows kind, trees blossom, grass sprouts on the pavements — and then the grass is wheat, there are crowds, a computer game, a film, a window — and then no one, and a fierce winter. The hallucination assembles and collapses in five quatrains, and the first line comes back as the last with a comic groan: “A-a-a, I won’t sniff lacquer any more.” A cautionary tale that refuses to preach.

## 29. Willfulness
Eight lines of self-portrait. The speaker likes being clever, understanding everyone, laughing “with a lunar laugh” and letting nothing show — and would like it more if he met clever people more often, to talk seriously “about the truth of the universe” and, in a verb of his own coinage, to “crown a thought.” The vanity is admitted and the loneliness behind it is the point.

## 30. Reflection
“My mind is downcast — but what to do?” Two quatrains of comic despair: the soul “does not know the body,” the mind has tired, and God knows why — “maybe because every single person has worn it out.” The slang of the last line lands the joke and keeps the poem from taking its own gloom seriously.

## 31. I Don't Want…
Seven lines and a threat. The speaker does not want life to say “Go away,” to squeeze the contemplation out of his soul and leave him “one memory” for fate to feed on. The triple exclamation mark is a raised voice; the ellipses that follow are its collapse: “I don’t want… I myself… will go…” Whether the going is departure or death the poem does not say. It is the first of several poems here about leaving before one is sent away.

## 32. * * *
A four-line miniature. The mind is filled with “a malicious feeling” the speaker cannot beat with his mere being — and against it, one wish: to live, flourish and grow “not like a cactus in the desert, but like flowers in a field.” Survival against flourishing, in a single image.

## 33. Sadness
Feelings “heavy as stone,” a mind “shackled by society’s fetters.” The speaker refuses the life he is living — a flame that “fades with time instead of blazing” — and promises the soul “pure freedom,” the body release from human pain, and never again “a step into dirty water.” He will return to himself and “rise by the mind to the heavens.” The rebellion is Romantic in the old sense; the enemy, society, will return in *Lost Fate* and *You — Society*.

## 34. O God!!!
A prayer that could equally be addressed to God or to a lost beloved, and never says which. Thoughts blow “like wind,” feelings are “flat as glass,” yet the pleading is “hot.” The speaker will not throw the addressee out of his dreams — “I can’t, and I have no wish to” — and will carry repentance “far into space, to the heavens.” Then the plainest line in the section: “Sad. Somehow it’s always so.” The last line asks to be found, “for I am a lost soul.” Numbness and heat in the same voice.

## 35. Thirst
An allegory of a garden. Someone — “He,” never named — has dried the speaker’s inside; the “beautiful garden of happiness” is overgrown with tall, evil grass; only the soul, “dried up like the mind, but alive,” is left. The beloved is the spring: “your water will be healing.” Water it generously and the weeds will die, orchards bloom, “spring for ever and ever” set fruit — and the harvest will be hers alone. The poem’s faith is simple and stated plainly: kindness leaves a trace.

## 36. Lost Fate
A fable in long, prose-like lines. Fate — *Dolia*, feminine, a figure from Ukrainian folk belief — walks out of the speaker and wanders off across sands, swamps and forests. The soul runs after her through the dark with fear at its side and comes back empty; the body does not want to live without Fate, lives anyway, “and made thought out of nothing.” Life notices “the labour of art,” loves the body for it, and gives it back its Fate as a birthday present — whereupon the body leaves Earth altogether for a dimension “where dreaminess remained the main thing.” Art rewarded, society escaped: the key to the whole middle of the book.

## 37. I BELIEVE
“Give me strength, O my Angel” — a prayer with an ultimatum. The speaker is “still very young,” will grow strong in thought and “send my protest to the World” for treating another’s life as a game; he will mend the World, perhaps even love it “like the tender sounds of ancient lyres.” But if strength is not given, he will vanish “into a World of understanding, a World without words — mute.” The refrain returns three times, and the capitalised title is a creed set against a threat. Youthful messianism, with the door left open.

## 38. * * *
A mind “tormented to distraction” by everyday questions, stains left “before repentance” that will not come out, the wish to live vanishing the moment one does not know where to put “the soul’s acquired negative element.” Fate is “embroidered with unclean threads” — the image of the *rushnyk*, the embroidered towel — and it warns: “at least you, don’t become her child.” Then the turn, and the reason the collection exists: on the eve of grief, “the soul is gladdened by those still unsuccessful lines, written on white paper.”

## 39. man or Man
Three tercets and a scale of being. A man with neither wit nor reason “resembles a donkey”; a man with wit, to whom reason comes with difficulty, “may count himself a lion”; a man who has “caressed” both — and “never mind that he hasn’t read books” — is a Man, “for people, an IDEAL.” The whole idea is in the title’s lower and upper case, and in the two Russian words for the mind, *um* and *razum*, which every translation has to keep apart.

## 40. * * *
A four-line joke with a moral: there are “narrower people” and “wider people” in this World, “but it is not on the body’s thickness that their mind and their wish to think depend.” The book’s shortest poem.

## 41. I Would Like…
A love poem addressed to a homeland, and a rough one: “I’m mad about you, my stupid motherland.” The speaker is “flattered” by her game with the whole human crowd, she is his only one — “but how I’d like you to be smart.” The wish that follows is entirely earnest: music everywhere, joy on faces, “the soul embracing the World,” evil gone for ever. Tenderness and exasperation in the same voice — the tone of someone who cannot leave.

## 42. You — Society
The most direct of the poems against society, spoken with a capital *I*. “You want to make me complain about life!? I won’t, I won’t!” Society tramples everything that grows, puts “desire, strength and spirit, will and honour” to sleep — “to sleep, to sleep, to sleep” — plucks out the eyes of those who still see, stuffs the body with tow “and calls this scarecrow — sighted!?!” The speaker will rise “from weakness.” The last stanza is quieter and sadder: all are “like stone, like sand, like clay… like a society, not like a person.”

## 43. SUCCESS IS NO SIN
Seven lines set in capitals like a slogan. The World is beautiful “in its greatness, and clear, though not for everyone”; to someone in it, “BIG SUCCESS” is destined by fate — “and this is by no means A SIN.” The Russian title rhymes *uspekh* with *grekh*; the poem is an aphorism defending ambition against the suspicion that it is shameful. Its companion is *Secret*, later in the book, which says what success costs.

## 44. Darkness
Eight lines, and the capital letter again: Man is great, “and great is his river of soul” — but not always, “for there is a man” with neither conscience nor shame, “nor even the word Kindness,” whose life is “like a black cat’s” and for whom “everything around is darkness.” A miniature portrait of the person the previous poems have been arguing with.

## 45. Have a Cry
Three quatrains of permission. “When you feel bad and don’t want to live — if you want, of course, you can cry.” Want to cry — cry; want to live — live; can’t see good — look, and “take happiness from life.” Grief is to be quelled “with soul and with a tear.” The gentlest poem in the section, written as advice to someone else and, one suspects, to oneself.

## 46. Melancholy
The Russian word *toska* — an ache without object, famously untranslatable — is repeated until it becomes a sound. It is longing for the sun and the skies, and above all for childhood: “bright multicoloured dreams,” puddles run through without discomfort. Then the image that carries the poem: melancholy “like a horseman” has bridled the speaker’s thoughts and given them “a grey-semantic tint” — a coined adjective — “reminding me that I am no longer a child.” Nostalgia, diagnosed.

## 47. Definition
Five quatrains of moral logic. Believe that evil will come, and it comes; believe “without doubt” only in good, and life will be pleasant; doubt, not knowing what to wish for, and “there will be nowhere to go when they come to take you.” Two destinations follow, light and dark, in the plainest terms. The title is the argument: what you believe *defines* where you end up. The poem’s confidence is its youth and its charm.

## 48. It's Sad When…
The birds fly to *vyriy* — the warm land beyond the sea where, in Ukrainian folk belief, they winter — taking with them “the joy of warm summer evenings and the beauty of untouched groves.” Summer waits there for winter “to play with the sun on the ice”; spring brings the birds back with song; the earth greens; and then the wheel turns again, and the last stanza repeats the first, now in the future tense. A poem about cycles, built as a cycle. The stray Latin letter in the title is the author’s own.

## 49. Without a State
The orchards bloom by the house and mother would call the children home — “but where? we have no house, no mother: we are orphans.” The private image opens into the public one: a people without a state of its own, an uncle who won glory “for a foreign State” and died of cold and hunger. “Why do we need a foreign state? Why do orphans need a foreign mother? No!” The cherry orchard by the house is Shevchenko’s, and the poem knows it; the last stanza is a condition rather than a promise: the orchards will bloom again “only when you, at least, do not betray your native state.”

## 50. * * *
“O Yes! O No!” — the poem begins by contradicting itself. Is what the speaker sees “a long strange dream,” or “nothing, compared with a dream” — the Russian has two words, *son* and *mechta*, and the poem lives between them. Years will pass and nothing will be remembered; then, hiding “the Proud Stray” inside his life, he will “give my mind to my people” and so light “an unburnable candle.” A civic vow arriving out of confusion, with a personification no one has explained.

## 51. Ramblings
The title is slang for a wild, nonsensical trip, and the poem begins as one: a mosquito swatted, a giant bee felled with a thrown hammer, a black horse commandeered. Then the ride turns into a tour of Ukrainian literature — the mother’s embroidered *rushnyk* from Malyshko’s song, the village grieving for Shevchenko’s Kateryna, the old kobzar in a field who says there is no happiness in Ukraine, “and Will and Fate only in a dream.” “No, uncle — it’s you who’s dreaming; Fate and Will are already here.” He smiles and vanishes, and the mother’s towel appears. Nonsense that ends as a patriotic vision, or the other way round.

## 52. The Little Bird Wanted…
A fable in eleven lines. A little bird by the window wants to fly to the sun; flies a day, flies two; “probably didn’t know the sun is far”; sweats, and burns. “Better if the bird hadn’t wanted…” The last lines drop from Icarus into the nursery: it didn’t get there, probably because “by the window it ate too little porridge.” The spaced-out punctuation is the author’s, and so is the bathos.

## 53. Question Mark?
An anti-smoking poem shaped, on the page, like its own title: the lines drift rightward and curl. Why take a cigarette? Better to eat “a tasteless sweet,” or go on holiday without money to “far-for-someone seaside Crimea.” Perhaps you smoke “to discover the unknown for yourself” — then don’t smoke much, stop, “and eat a sweet.” The coda, for those on a diet, forbids the cigarette as well. Advice with a wink, laid out as a drawing.

## 54. Alconaut
The title welds “alcoholic” to “cosmonaut,” and the poem is an apostrophe in three stanzas whose epithet keeps shifting by a single sound: *pale, pale* — *pale, poor* — *pale, harmful*. Why does the man “shine with pallor,” why “leave for the alcohol age,” why lie “at the bottom like a cobblestone” of a dried river? He is silent; he cannot look at himself. The last line is not a rebuke but pity: “What a pity… what a pity for you!”

## 55. The Bird and the Birds
A spring morning, a bench, and three glances upward. First, birds circling, “needing nothing more.” Second, above the natural birds, “another bird — the one we made.” Third, no birds at all: only “transparent boundlessness,” into which the speaker “flew off in thought.” Nature, machine, mind — three altitudes in four quatrains, and a calm epiphany that the closing poem of the section, *O.Rich*, will turn into a flight.

## 56. I Love to Love
A hymn built on one verb. The speaker loves the warm spring morning, the dawn, the ray that “breaks the tight curtain of non-being”; loves freedom, desire, joy and beauty; loves “to remain myself.” Loves to love, loves to feel love — “of course I love. But why?.. I don’t know…” The shrug at the end is honest, and it is answered much later, in *Love as Truth*, by a poem that begins with the same words and knows the reason.

## 57. Early in the Morning
Two stanzas of stepped lines and one image: the sky as a sea the speaker would like to drown in. Its depths “lured me into searching” at dawn; the distance is “beautiful and enchanting, and I live as nothing else does”; at the edge of “unknown being” the sun rises and the soul is “taken captive by feeling.” A small, pure dawn poem — the least troubled voice in the section.

## 58. O.Rich
The section ends with a vision in five stanzas, each opened by a single stressed word — *Dream. Night. Moment. World. Taste.* — that rhymes into the lines that follow. Grape clusters, a spring orchard, flax, an unnoticed bird; then the earth face to face with the speaker under a moon, and a name written in the sky in Latin letters: “O.Rich — my name was born in the sky.” He becomes the bird, ignores the earth crying “Come back!”, sees a freedom “without sky and Earth” — and wakes to winter, black earth, and a one-word English curse. The only wish left is to go back into the dream for good. Who or what O.Rich is, the poem does not explain; it is a self the poet has just met.

## 59. * * *
*Love as Drama* opens with hair on the lips, hands in hands, two hearts “in the rhythm of passion,” a star burning for two — “my beautiful Lady, my Feeling.” Then, without warning, “I was near — and suddenly I am not here,” and a hot tear on her cheek. The speaker promises to return “through other worlds” and carry her “into eternity in my arms,” and the first line comes back as the last. A ring closed around an absence; the whole drama of the section in five stanzas.

## 60. Contrast
A poem of definitions set against each other, as the title promises. Thought is a cobweb in which a frightened child hides — “pray for her.” Purity of soul is the source of beauty; dirt and darkness “become familiar with time.” Solitude is “the greatest joy,” an edge to flee to from society. Boundlessness filled with love is a wound “flooded with hot blood,” and the pain is the salt in the tear. Suffering is a way of existing in “that ardent game we once called love — and then gave it all our dreams.” Philosophy in rhymed quatrains, with the last line as the wound.

## 61. For You, Kitten
A litany of *why*: why did God give the joy of love, why do thoughts overflow the mind, why did I touch your hand, why did the fire catch if it can never go out? The pet name in the title — *kitten* — sits inside metaphysical questions, and that is the point: the loss is intimate and total at once. “My soul is my child in love — why are you alive? Die!” Body, eyes, tears, words, space, freedom, money and fame are all asked what they are for, and the last question is the speaker himself: “Why am I..? if you are not there — why am I..?”

## 62. Madame
The formal *Vy* again, and a suit that forbids itself. “Madame, I want you in every sense — you are so sweet that I cannot propose.” The pleasures are catalogued with old-fashioned care: birdsong, the bass of a waterfall, a smile “without an admixture of unfreedom,” eyes like the gleam of a ruby, features that are “a reflection of a dream, like space for a fledgling.” She is so light, so beautiful, that he “will not allow himself” to offer his hand and heart, “though I shall have to live in suffering.” Nineteenth-century courtesy in a very modern first line.

## 63. Don't Over…
The title breaks off — *don’t over-* what? — and the poem is a wish-list, each stanza beginning “Be for me”: a muse “sounding like music, in gentle adjacent notes”; a flower of eternal spring; a game that captivates “only not a shock”; a nanny, a toy, a reader of the children’s magazine *Malyatko*, “because I’m still a little child.” The last request is the only serious one and undoes the rest: “above all, with me, always be yourself.” The musical *notes* of the first stanza echo the title of the collection.

## 64. Remember
Five apostrophes, each to a different name for the same person: my happiness, my sun, bright star, my feeling, my fate. “Paint me warmth, blue sky, space and freedom… paint us red among the grey people.” Her light is “the life of my eyes”; feeling’s limit is infinity — “you have wings: fly.” The last address is a demand — “Say it!!! Do you no longer know me?” — and the answer, if it comes, is “how silence sings.” An ode in the high style, ending on the collection’s musical note.

## 65. Is It Too Late?
A dialogue of mistimed love, built out of sentences the speaker cannot finish: “when I already…” Once, courting her, he was told “Where is love? Show me — I don’t see it. Aha! Tell me… it’s all dre-e-eams,” and went home saying to the sky that he loved her. Now she loves him, and he will not be silent: “I don’t want, don’t want, don’t want!” — though he will not forget her. The poem answers the untitled *If you do not love me* from the first section, where the same question, *show me where love is*, was his. What to do “when I already… no…” is left, deliberately, unsaid.

## 66. Don't Hover
An unrhymed manifesto, the only free verse in the section. Don’t count on me as property; count on me as a friend. Don’t demand that I be obliged to you, or become your captive “losing my own thought.” Don’t do for me what you don’t really want to do; don’t live for me “if you haven’t learned to live for yourself.” The last stanza gathers the three refusals into a condition and turns them inside out: “Don’t count on me, don’t demand from me, don’t do for me — if you want me.” Love defined by what it will not accept.

## 67. And Where Are You Going?
*Theatrum mundi*, with Greek letters. The river of daily events carries the speaker through “priceless youth” — “I’m an Actor! It’s only a stage!” — but the set changes, the role changes, and the refrain keeps asking: “But where am I?” Face-masks, foreign cities, voices “like the barking of dogs,” a new play in which “he is α again, you are γ again,” then 3 and 5. Someone calls for the Main HERO — “isn’t that my role?” Everyone hurries, pushes, calls — “NO — that is my answer!” The one who left the public path did not go where everyone went. An existential poem that ends in a single capitalised refusal.

## 68. A Question of Eternity
A late autumn evening, dusk “on the city’s shoulders,” the day’s bustle carried off “to the Kingdom of Sleep.” Tonight and tomorrow are a locked labyrinth, a rebus, a code; life is “the axiom of the medal’s two sides.” Then the speaker addresses Life directly: how to live “so as to live, and not just stupidly be”? Am I living, or merely alive? The answer is a bequest hidden inside a philosophical poem: if the thoughts are alive, the poems will remain — “poetry and prose — this is for You: my family and my crew.” The last word is slang, and it is the tenderest in the poem.

## 69. No Judge
A scene, almost a play: an old man and a boy begging for change. “What do you need, boy? Bread for your brother in some basement, who has shot up for the last time?” Or glue and a plastic bag “for yourself and your friends”? The boy will not listen — “no time for chatter” — and runs off “looking for the way to the latrine.” Then the old man calls him back and offers his own breakfast, “sorry it’s without sausage, take apples too” — and the boy does not need bread. The final stanza repeats an earlier one with the verbs shifted into the past: nothing has changed. No one is judged; the title is the poem’s whole restraint.

## 70. Big Boy
The longest poem in *Love as Drama*. A boy at a window sees “the truths behind it,” his mouth shut while “the boy’s soul sobs.” Night calls him — “come, all the delights are ahead” — and the poem stops him: remember what you saw. Below, in the night city, a man lay not breathing; men and women passed by “lustfully” — “this is the truth of ‘grown-ups’”; the ambulance took the body into the dark, and the boy’s heart burst. “Cry, boy, cry! Tears are your doctor today… Would a strong one cry as you do?” No one hears him. “You are strong, boy, you are a soul — this is the first truth of your life.” Growing up as the discovery that crying is strength.

## 71. * * *
“I want to grab hold of life” — four times, at the head of four stanzas. For the moment of being young, for the fire in the heart, for “the child inside who makes fantasy fly”; to stop time and bring happiness into being — “or is this wish an unfulfilled dream, smoke?” The last stanza knows the answer and wants anyway: there is no disappointment yet, “I’m still alive”; the repentance will be bitter “all the same,” but “still I want — while I’m young.” Carpe diem, spoken by someone who can already see the end of the day.

## 72. Secret
The secret is labour. “You must work no matter what, and this work will bring you everything” — on yourself, “the inner and the outer, the body and the soul.” Not he is successful who reached success and “remained sinful, and fame is his amusement,” but he who works. The first stanza returns at the end in the third person, closing the ring. Read with *SUCCESS IS NO SIN* from the first section: there ambition was defended; here its price is named.

## 73. I'll Stay Alone
Short lines, a calm voice, and a decision: to stay alone and “be like the wind, even if I must answer with death.” Nothing matters — yesterday, tomorrow, society, renunciation — “when you die suddenly.” Only the soul, which leaves the body lightly into space; let it merge “as a drop and the ocean,” and no one will provoke deception any more. The image of the drop and the sea is old and mystical; the poem uses it not to mourn but to stop struggling.

## 74. To Stand One's Ground
“I can’t sleep — I must know who I am, where I am and why, where is my native mother, where the house, where sinless being.” The mother is *nenka*, a word Ukrainians also use for the country itself, and the house has a boundless well of healing water to drink from and draw. Only then, thirst quenched, can one show courage, “truth, will and honour before the one who carries bloody death inside him.” Three five-line stanzas that move from insomnia to homecoming to resolve. Water, from *Thirst*; home, from *Without a State*.

## 75. UNDERSTAND
The last poem of *Love as Drama* turns toward the section that follows. Life is a game — but a game “without shield and sword” is “not worth the candle.” What matters is not to give death or take life, but to fill the emptiness in other souls “with the Perfect,” and to heal. And when the wish to live goes and one cannot forgive oneself? “Don’t perish — pray! Seek, and you will find” the paths to where “you will understand that they are not all lying — and find the meaning of life.” The gospel echo is deliberate; the capitals of the title are a hand on the shoulder.

## 76. Who?
*Love as Truth* opens with a riddle in similes. Like a gull over the sea, like wind in the leaves, like mountains, like the lion over the beasts; “pleasant-light and beautiful,” “piercing and all-containing,” “highest when low”; like sun after long rain and rain after drought, “like hands for the armless”; like boundless space, like a sea with no bottom, like the ideal, like perfection — “like the One for Whom I am for ever.” No subject is named until the capital letter of the last line. The title asks the question; the poem’s four quatrains are the answer, withheld until it can no longer be anything else.

## 77. Choose the Light
The title is a Ukrainian verb with a Russian noun — the author’s two languages in three words. “Is life beautiful?” a person asks, and “against himself, in vain,” answers no. Life without God is “total darkness”; “God is the word, the word is light,” and whoever understands the word will not say no. Don’t hide, don’t run; accept the word, “and do not perish.” Life is beautiful, “nothing is more beautiful” — and choosing death “puts out the bright light in vain.” A hymn in plain quatrains, and the collection’s clearest answer to its own darkest poems.

## 78. * * *
“I love to live, I love life” — the words of *I Love to Love* from the first section, which ended “But why?.. I don’t know.” Here the *why* has a name: the Creator, “Holy Spirit, God the Father and His Son, Jesus Christ.” The speaker accepts “the blood shed by God for me,” loves to live “in the delight of knowing Truth and Essence, not good and evil,” and chooses life. The short two-beat lines fall like a litany, and the last two are advice: “So as not to die — do not choose evil.”

## 79. And Why Do You…?
A testimony in a conversational key: “You know…? I love God.” Disappointment and anxiety are therefore “not real, but empty, like the reality in a dream”; someone else’s dreams have gone out in silence, “but not mine, for I am for Him.” The reason is given without theology: because He forgives “again and again,” lifts one from the knees “and teaches reason like a small child.” The speaker admits “so many different words — maybe you understood nothing” and says it once more. The title turns the confession into a question for the reader: *and why do you…?*

## 80. To All Poets
The envoi of *Before*. “Poets, tell us please, if it isn’t hard, how to become a poet.” How to put thoughts in “a poetic form, simple and usual for you”; how to convey beauty, desire, joy and freedom in verse; how to drive “life’s discord” from the soul; how thoughts “flew up into the sky with the birds” — the transparent boundlessness of *The Bird and the Birds* again. Tercets, modest and a little mischievous: “tell us without arrogance, dear poets — well, at least a stanza each.” An apprentice’s petition, placed at the end of eighty poems that have already answered it.
